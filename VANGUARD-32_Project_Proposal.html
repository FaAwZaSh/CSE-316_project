VANGUARD‑32

Autonomous Smart Perimeter Defense & Active Countermeasure Platform — a multi-sensor-fusion targeting turret built around the ATmega32.
PLATFORM: ATmega32‑16PU DURATION: 6 Weeks TEAM SIZE: 3 Members STATUS: Proposal · Rev 1.0
01

Motivation

Single-sensor security systems trigger too often on nothing and verify nothing before they do — VANGUARD-32 fixes that with layered, automated confirmation.

Problem Description

Conventional perimeter security relies on a single sensor type — usually a passive infrared (PIR) detector or an optical tripwire. These devices cannot distinguish a person from wind-blown debris, a stray animal, or a temperature swing, so they generate frequent false alarms. Worse, they are purely passive: they cannot re-orient themselves toward the disturbance, confirm that a real, physically-present target exists, or respond without a human operator watching a monitor around the clock.

Why This Problem Matters

Remote installations — substations, warehouses, border outposts, industrial yards — often have no on-site guard. A system that can detect, verify, and react on its own removes the human-latency bottleneck that lets real intrusions go unanswered for minutes at a time, while also cutting the false-alarm fatigue that causes real operators to start ignoring alerts altogether.

Why Our Group Selected This Project

VANGUARD-32 is not one sensor demo bolted onto an MCU — it is a complete embedded engineering problem. It forces us to combine real-time multi-sensor fusion, closed-loop dual-axis actuation, three simultaneous communication buses (I²C, SPI, UART), and sub-millisecond interrupt handling, all on an 8-bit, resource-constrained ATmega32. That breadth is exactly what this course is meant to test.

Main Objectives

01 · Sector Triangulation

Detect intrusion vectors across a 180° field split into three independent sectors using low-latency hardware interrupts (no polling).

02 · Pan-Tilt Acquisition

Slew a 2-axis turret toward the triggered sector within <500 ms using hardware PWM.

03 · Multi-Modal Verification

Confirm a real physical target via IR thermal delta (ΔT) and laser time-of-flight distance (R) over I²C before trusting the alarm.

04 · Wireless IFF Handshake

Authenticate personnel carrying an encrypted RF badge over SPI to prevent friendly engagements.

05 · Active Countermeasure

Trigger an automated deterrence sequence (strobe / solenoid) only after a hostile target is confirmed.

06 · Telemetry & Black Box

Stream a live tactical radar view to a PC over UART while logging every incident to a MicroSD card.

System Design

The ATmega32 sits at the centre of three sensing spokes, two actuation spokes, and two communication spokes. Every arrow below is a real electrical bus, not a conceptual link.

FIG. 1 — VANGUARD-32 SYSTEM BLOCK DIAGRAM

Role of Each Component

  • Sensing subsystem: Three PIR/RCWL sector sensors fire hardware interrupts the instant motion crosses a zone — zero polling overhead. MLX90614 and VL53L0X then independently confirm the target is warm and physically solid before anything is trusted as real.
  • Actuation subsystem: Pan/tilt servos re-aim the sensor head at the triggered sector; the MOSFET-driven strobe/solenoid executes the deterrence response only after verification passes.
  • Communication & storage subsystem: nRF24L01 runs the IFF challenge-response over SPI; MicroSD logs every event; the FTDI bridge streams live telemetry to the PC radar GUI.

Role of the ATmega32

The ATmega32 is not a passenger reading pre-processed data — it owns every stage of the decision loop, from raw interrupt to final actuation.

FIG. 2 — ATmega32 FINITE STATE MACHINE

Interrupts & GPIO

INT0/INT1/INT2 catch sector triggers in sub-millisecond time with zero CPU polling. GPIO lines drive the two SPI chip-selects and the countermeasure MOSFET gate.

Timers & PWM

Timer0 & Timer2 generate independent 50 Hz PWM on OC0/OC2 for pan and tilt servos. Timer1 (16-bit) runs as a free-running microsecond clock for non-blocking task scheduling.

I²C (TWI)

One 100 kHz bus shared by MLX90614 (0x5A), VL53L0X (0x29), and the status LCD — the ATmega32 arbitrates all three as bus master.

SPI

4 MHz master-mode bus multiplexed between nRF24L01 (low-latency IFF packets) and MicroSD (bulk sector writes), separated by software-controlled CS lines with explicit release routines.

USART

115200 baud continuous telemetry frames ($ANGLE,DIST,TEMP,STATUS#) feeding the live PC radar GUI.

ADC

Single-channel (ADC0) supply-voltage monitor to flag brown-out risk before servo current spikes cause a reset.

Use of Other Controllers — Optional

The ATmega32 is the sole main microcontroller executing all core logic, sensor reads, motor control, storage logging, and telemetry — it is not offloading any primary task.

Auxiliary Node: IFF Wearable Badge

Why required: The badge must be a small, mobile, battery-powered node physically separate from the turret station — this cannot be the same chip running the turret.

Controller used: Arduino Nano / ATtiny85.

Tasks performed: Listens for a 2.4 GHz challenge packet from the ATmega32 turret via its own nRF24L01 module and replies with a pre-shared 32-bit authorization hash. It performs no other system function.

Communication with ATmega32: Strictly wireless, over-the-air 2.4 GHz RF packets — no wired link exists between the badge and the main turret.

Challenges & Future Scope

Technical & Hardware Challenges

SPI Contention

Sharing the SPI bus between the low-latency nRF24L01 and the bulk-write MicroSD module can cause lockups.

Mitigation: dedicated software-controlled CS lines with explicit bus-release routines.

Inductive Noise

Servo current draw during rapid repositioning can dip the supply and brown-out the MCU.

Mitigation: isolated 5V/6V rails from separate buck converters, plus 1000μF bulk decoupling.

Blocking I²C

Slow sensor response times could stall the main control loop if read as blocking calls.

Mitigation: state-driven non-blocking I²C polling instead of fixed delays.

Future Scope

Computer Vision

ESP32-CAM co-processor for AI-based facial recognition and visual target classification.

360° Slewing

Continuous stepper motors + slip rings to replace the 180° servo limit for full perimeter tracking.

LoRa Mesh

Long-range telemetry upgrade for multi-kilometer networking across several deployed units.
